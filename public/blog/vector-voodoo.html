How Cosine Similarity Cracks the Text Comparison Code

July 28, 2024 / 4 min read Read on Substack ↗

Geometry often wields the power to simplify complex ideas. When it comes to measuring similarity in text analysis, cosine similarity consistently makes the list. But what exactly is it? Let's dive into the angle of understanding….

By definition

“Cosine similarity is a mathematical metric used to measure the similarity between two vectors in a multi-dimensional space, particularly in high-dimensional spaces, by calculating the cosine of the angle between them.”

So to simplify, it measures the similarity in the direction or orientation of the vectors ignoring differences in their magnitude or scale. Both vectors need to be part of the same inner product space, meaning they must produce a scalar through inner product multiplication. The similarity of two vectors is measured by the cosine of the angle between them.

Imagine there are three sentences :

1- apple is a fruit.

2- orange is a fruit.

3- banana is a berry.

and if we vectorize these sentences by a simple countvectorizer we can get vectors like 

a=[1 0 0 1 1 0] b=[0 0 0 1 1 1] c=[0 1 1 0 1 0]

each signifying one sentence and then apply the cosine similarity mathematics which tells :

cosine(θ) = a.b /( |a|*|b| )

where a.b is dot product of vectors and |a| & |b| are magnitudes (Euclidean norms) of vectors . The result ranges from -1 to 1, where:

  • 1 indicates perfect similarity (0° angle)

  • 0 indicates orthogonality (90° angle)

  • -1 indicates perfect dissimilarity (180° angle)

In most text analysis applications, we deal with non-negative values, so the range is typically [0, 1].

when we apply it and compare 1 with 2 or 1 with 3 we get values like :

0.6666666666666667
0.33333333333333337

Which shows that sentence 1 and 2 are more similar than 1 and 3, which is also evident from reading the sentences. Below is a simple code that implements this, and you can also use the cosine similarity function, which is directly available in the sklearn library.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer, TfidfVectorizer

sentences = ['apple is a fruit.', 'orange is a fruit.', 'banana is a berry.']

s = CountVectorizer()

t = TfidfVectorizer()

vectors = s.fit_transform(sentences).toarray()

vec = t.fit_transform(sentences).toarray()

a, b, c = vectors[0], vectors[1], vectors[2]

x, y, z = vec[0], vec[1], vec[2]

print(a, b, c)

def calculate_similarity(v1, v2):

dot = np.dot(v1, v2)

n_v1 = np.linalg.norm(v1)

n_v2 = np.linalg.norm(v2)

cos = dot / (n_v1 * n_v2) if n_v1 and n_v2 else 0

return cos

cosinea_b = calculate_similarity(a, b)

cosineb_c = calculate_similarity(b, c)

cosinea_c = calculate_similarity(a, c)

print(cosinea_b, cosinea_c, cosineb_c)

print(x, y, z)

cosinex_y = calculate_similarity(x, y)

cosiney_z = calculate_similarity(y, z)

cosinex_z = calculate_similarity(x, z)

print(cosinex_y, cosiney_z, cosinex_z)

There are many different ways to vectorize sentences and words that capture semantics and context more accurately, such as Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText, and others.

Now that you have understood how cosine similarity works and the mathematics behind it,You might wonder:

"If vectors are represented in 2D or 3D, we get a close look at the angles between them or euclidean distance which often tells us what is more similar. Then why don't we use it directly? Why the need for cosine and all this mathematics?"

The answer lies in the nature of the data we're often dealing with:

  1. Most real-world data (like text) is high-dimensional, making direct angle visualization or distance calculation less informative.

  2. Many dimensions may be zero, making Euclidean distance less meaningful.

  3. Cosine similarity focuses on direction, not magnitude, which is often more important in similarity comparisons.

Consider two text documents that have the same words but different frequencies. Euclidean distance might declare them dissimilar due to the magnitude difference, while cosine similarity would recognize their content similarity based on the angle between their vectors.

When applying cosine similarity to text data in NLP, preprocessing steps like stemming and lemmatization are crucial:

Stemming is the process of shortening words to their basic form, often using simple rules. For example, the words "running," "runs," and "ran" can all be reduced to "run".

Lemmatization is a more sophisticated process that reduces words to their base i.e dictionary form , considering the context and part of speech. For instance, "better" would be lemmatized to "good".

These techniques are important for cosine similarity because they help to:

  1. Reduce dimensionality by consolidating related words.

  2. Improve similarity measures by treating variations of the same word as identical.

  3. Handle inflected forms and derivationally related words consistently.
