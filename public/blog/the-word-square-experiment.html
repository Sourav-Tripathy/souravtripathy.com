The Word Square Experiment

A look into the hood of reasoning in LLMs

March 11, 2025 / 6 min read Read on Substack ↗

Word games have always been a part of me—Scrabble, crosswords, anything that makes me sift through my brain systematically, connecting ideas.

Back in 9th grade, a math teacher introduced me to word squares—grids where the words in the i-th row and j-th column match when i = j. Seemed simple at first. I managed 3×3 grids easily, but 5×5 grids were brutal. After an hour of struggling, I built a couple, draining my vocabulary dry and When I switched to 7x7, my mind went blank..

A few days ago, while playing Scrabble, an idea hit me—LLMs have reasoning now, so why not test them on word squares? I ran an experiment with ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Gemini, and Claude, pushing them to construct word squares. The results? Fascinating. Here's how they did and what I think about them.

P.S- I assumed they already know what is a word square because when asked they gave the correct definition..

Prompt - “Build me a word square of 3 x 3 and all the words in it should be valid English words with a meaning.”

Observations Across Different Models

Common Patterns and Model Behaviour

3 x 3 Grid

  • ChatGPT and DeepSeek generated words randomly and then validated them using the rules of a word square. Their approach resembled a brute-force method but with a crucial realisation that picking the first word matters significantly.

  • Gemini, on the other hand, followed a combinatoric approach—thinking deeply about common nouns, verbs, and adjectives, exploring multiple paths before arriving at an answer. However, it eventually hallucinated, incorrectly claiming its answer was valid, yet when it stepped out of the reasoning phase, it surprisingly provided a correct response.

  • Claude was fast and created a word square correctly in very less time compared to others.

  • Fascinatingly, all four models—ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Gemini and Claude—at some point independently selected "CAT" as one of the words, hinting at either a statistical pattern in language models or something deeper in their training data.

5 x 5 Grid

  • ChatGPT and DeepSeek converged on "HEART" as the initial word. DeepSeek’s reasoning process initially seemed methodical, but in the middle, it got lost and suddenly "recalled" five words that fit—suggesting that either training data influence or may be MoE architecture played a role in this unexpected memory-like behaviour.

  • ChatGPT struggled to find valid English words and eventually resorted to anagrams and Latin words used in English. This led it to the famous "Sator Square" one of the oldest known word squares. However, this seemed more like a retrieval from its training data than an original discovery. When asked to produce another valid square apart from Sator, it failed, acknowledging the difficulty.

  • Gemini took a different approach—systematically working through combinatorics—but seemed to overthink, leading to an infinite loop where it repeatedly used "needly" and "need" for hundreds of iterations.Despite this, its systemic approach closely mimics how humans think, though it does not self-check efficiently to correct errors early.

  • Claude displayed extreme hallucination—producing words like "RLSET," which have no real meaning but still obeyed the structure of a word square. This raises the question: was it simply generating valid letter patterns without checking for meaning?

7 x 7 Grid

  • ChatGPT outright admitted the challenge and failed to generate a word square.

  • Gemini confidently provided an incorrect answer, indicating unchecked hallucination.

  • DeepSeek, rather than hallucinating, attempted to fill up the grid but surrendered when it couldn't fully complete the structure—an interesting approach of partial correctness instead of confident hallucination.

  • Claude once again hallucinated and confidently provided a wrong answer, reinforcing its tendency to favour structural correctness over semantic accuracy.

Human vs. LLM Thinking: A Comparative Insight

Interestingly, when I asked two friends—a coder and a non-coder—to attempt the 3x3 word square:

  • Both followed a similar approach: picking the first word randomly and then using combinatoric reasoning to find the rest.

  • The coder, however, had an intuitive bias—choosing a word containing "E" or "A" due to their frequency in English. This suggests that reasoning skills differ based on domain expertise, and since LLMs are RL-trained on validated text, they exhibit similar biases by favouring common letters.

Why This Happens: Understanding Model Behaviour

  1. Auto regressive Nature & Error Propagation

    • Since LLMs predict the next token based on previous ones, an initial incorrect choice amplifies errors in subsequent predictions. This explains why models like Claude and Gemini sometimes go off the rails, producing structurally correct but meaningless outputs.It makes me support the inherent bottleneck that exists auto-regressive LLMs.

  2. Self-Checking & Iterative Refinement

    • DeepSeek exhibits an interesting behaviour—it realises errors mid-way and starts over. This resembles a form of self-checking, a positive trait that prevents it from confidently asserting incorrect outputs like Gemini does.

  3. Training Data Influence

    • The repeated occurrence of words like "HEART" and "CAT" across different models suggests these might be common in training datasets related to word games. This raises an interesting question: Are LLMs truly reasoning, or are they leveraging learned statistical patterns from prior data?

  4. Different Approaches

    • ChatGPT’s fallback to anagram-based solutions and Latin words hints at a retrieval-like behaviour, while DeepSeek’s sudden "recall" of five correct words suggests a different mechanism—potentially because of MoE (Mixture of Experts) .

    • Gemini, though structured, seems to over-optimise its reasoning process—causing infinite loops unless cut off by system constraints.(I think)

    • Claude prioritizes structural correctness over meaning, resulting in highly confident hallucinations. It focuses on fitting answers to the format and type required by the user's query rather than ensuring semantic accuracy. In its attempt to complete the task, it forcefully inserts words into the grid while providing different words in the description, creating a misleading impression of correctness.

Final Takeaways

  • Picking the first word is crucial—whether humans or models, this initial step significantly impacts the solution path.

  • Some models favour retrieval, others favour generative combinatorics—GPT and DeepSeek leverage memorisation when struggling, while Gemini sticks to systematic approach but risks infinite loops.

  • Self-correction is key—DeepSeek exhibits iterative reasoning, preventing runaway hallucinations, whereas Gemini goes too deep and Claude confidently hallucinates. Deepseek and Gemini are similar in reasoning style but Deepseek having self correction and iterative reasoning prevents it from going too much into nonsense area.

  • Training data likely plays a role—common words like "HEART" and "CAT" appear independently across models, suggesting preexisting linguistic biases or existence of such words more frequently in the word games data part in the training set may be.

  • Brute force was present in all, even if disguised. Gemini seemed methodical, but it ultimately relied on systematic trial and error rather than logic-based pruning of bad choices.

  • As the problem scaled, models failed more drastically, revealing a lack of true recursive reasoning that humans naturally develop.

  • LLMs demonstrate an iterative approach to problem-solving. They propose potential solutions, evaluate them against the rules of the word square, and then refine or discard them based on the results. This trial-and-error process is central to their reasoning.However, unlike humans, they do not backtrack effectively. Instead of reconsidering earlier choices, they often get stuck in local loops or hallucinate solutions.
