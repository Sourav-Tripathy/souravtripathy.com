A Stillness That Holds You

Published on May 17, 2025 • Read on Substack

Some films change how you see cinema. Columbus changes how you see life.

It’s not a film you watch. It’s a film you feel, wander into, sit with. It moves slowly, because it’s about what happens when nothing moves. It speaks in stillness, geometry, breath. It gives you space…not to escape, but to face yourself. In Columbus, life doesn’t rush at you. It grows from you.

This is how I see it. I don’t know what the director meant. But this is what it feels like.

The Opening Frame:Grief in Quiet Corners

Jin enters through a window, a hallway, a phone call. His father, a scholar of architecture, lies in a coma, suspended between life and death. There’s no loud grief, no tears. Just a man standing in a hospital’s sterile light, his silhouette sharp against the glass. The film doesn’t throw us into pain. It lets us stand at its edge, watching.

Columbus, Indiana, unfolds. The town is not a backdrop. It’s a mirror. Mid-century buildings - clean, symmetrical, deliberate…stand like silent witnesses. The Irwin Union Bank, its glass walls gleaming. The Miller House, its grids open and calm. The North Christian Church, its spire piercing the sky. They don’t fix anything. But they hold space, offer clarity, like a book you open when the world feels heavy.

Then we meet Casey. She’s young, fierce, still. She works in a library, guides architecture tours for kids. She’s not aimless, but anchored - by her mother, a recovering addict, by this town, by the beauty others miss. Her stillness isn’t weakness. It’s love. Haley Lu Richardson’s eyes flicker with hope, fear, quiet strength. She’s a Kafka character, trapped yet resilient, finding meaning in the ordinary.

The Library Scene:Meaning Lives in You

Jin and Casey talk about architecture, but it’s not about architecture. It’s about seeing each other.

At the library, designed by Eliel Saarinen, Casey doesn’t recite facts. She shares a memory: as a child, walking into this space, its high ceilings and soft light made her feel seen. It didn’t demand. It just was. Her hesitant smile, caught in the library’s glow, says more than her words. This is the film’s truth: buildings, like people, have presence. Some challenge you. Some hold you. Some show you yourself.

Jin, played by John Cho with guarded intensity, doesn’t know how to answer. He’s used to his father’s academic detachment, a world of theories, not feelings. But he listens, his silhouette softening against the library’s symmetry. A connection begins— not romance, but recognition.

Their first meeting, near a hospital fence, tells the story words don’t. A low brick wall divides them, its symmetry stark. They’re strangers, cautious, their voices halting. But as they walk, talk, open up, the fence fades from the frame. They stand side by side, the town’s clean lines behind them. It’s visual poetry, plain and true. The barrier is gone. So is the distance.

Lingering:The Weight of Staying

Lingering is the film’s heartbeat. Casey stays in Columbus, not because she can’t leave, but because she chooses her mother. In a quiet moment, she calls her, voice soft, eyes darting, checking if she’s safe. The fear of relapse lingers in her pause. It’s not inertia. It’s care.

Jin lingers too, but not by choice. His father’s coma holds him here, a duty he resents. In a hospital corridor, he talks to Eleanor, his father’s assistant, his voice sharp as he recalls a childhood eclipsed by lectures, not love. The corridor’s symmetry mocks him — cold, ordered, like the father he never knew. John Cho’s restrained gaze carries the weight of that absence.

This isn’t a story of breaking free. It’s a story of staying. In a world that praises leaving, Columbus honors tending to people, to pain, to places. The soundtrack, Hammock’s soft drones and delicate strings, hums like a pulse, its calm repetition mirroring the architecture’s symmetry. One night, in a car, Casey points to a plain building,striped, unremarkable…and says, “In the middle of all this, there’s this.” It’s not about the building. It’s about her anchor, her reason to hold on. We all have one : a place, a memory - that makes sense only to us.

Spatial Ethics:Framing the Soul

The camera is still. Frames are symmetrical, reverent. The Irwin Union Bank, its glass panes aligned. The Miller House, its grids warm with light. The North Christian Church, its spire centered against the sky. But within this stillness, emotions churn. Characters stand small against buildings, or cradled by them, never alone. The negative space isn’t empty. It holds.

Jin and Casey hold space too. They don’t touch, don’t confess. They simply are. In the Miller House, they wander, the camera gliding past red cushions on beige walls, green gardens beyond glass. The colors pop, like emotions breaking through restraint. They talk about design, but it’s about feeling…how a space can move you, change you. It’s a Stevenson moment, strangers finding solace in a shared glance.

By a river, they stand, a bridge’s arches rising behind them, their reflections rippling in the water. They try to name why a building feels alive. The conversation doesn’t end in answers, but in understanding. The bridge’s symmetry holds their asymmetrical lives…Jin’s drift, Casey’s anchor. It’s like a Murakami scene, where nature and structure collide, and meaning hovers.

At the bank, Casey speaks of its glass walls, how they make her feel exposed yet safe. When she quotes an architect, we see the building’s reflection. When she speaks her own truth, the camera cuts to her face, her voice trembling. The film honors her voice, her meaning. It says: what you feel is enough.

In one of the final shots, the North Christian Church stands, its symmetry perfect. But a tree’s shadow falls unevenly, disrupting the frame. It’s life…grief, growth, asymmetry — etched against order. Jin lingers, Casey is gone. The building remains, holding their story.

The Philosophy of Pause

Healing isn’t a burst. It’s a slow turning. Clarity isn’t a flood. It’s fragments, gathered over time. Columbus builds revelation like architecture - line by line, breath by breath. Stillness isn’t absence. It’s a canvas where emotions paint themselves.

The spaces between words matter as much as the words. Jin and Casey are often silent, not for lack of things to say, but to let the unsayable breathe. The soundtrack’s gentle hum, like the buildings, holds that silence, gives it weight.

The film doesn’t demand change. Jin lingers, not triumphant, but touched by understanding. Casey leaves, not defeated, but ready to move. There’s no neat arc. Just motion, or the will to move again.

Coda: Life as a Designed Space

Columbus isn’t a story. It’s a meditation. When Casey points to that striped building, finding beauty in its plainness, she’s building her own space - a life where meaning grows from within. The architecture, like her, doesn’t shout. It stands, quiet, intentional, a reminder of why we stay.

This is a love letter to a film, to a way of seeing. To noticing a building and feeling its pulse. To pausing with a stranger and finding connection. To holding space for the mess and calling it enough.

Columbus taught me that life isn’t a force that crashes in. It’s a structure we shape - moment by moment, line by line, breath by breath.

It doesn’t come at you.

It comes from you….

Anyone reading this, I urge you , please — watch this film. Let it sit with you. Let it unfold.
